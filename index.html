Foundation phase

What the photographs are telling us

The site is using a family of large, deep drilled foundations. The visible evidence is unusually rich: numbered steel casings, wet drilling, huge reinforcement cages, continuous concrete pumping, cut casing scrap and progressively deeper excavation around completed shafts.

45+

shaft IDs observed

The painted numbers behave like individual foundation identifiers. Seeing #45 makes a total in the 40–50+ range plausible, though the final count is not yet known.

Multiple sizes

not one standard foundation

Larger interior shafts and smaller perimeter shafts strongly suggest different design capacities and structural demands.

Wet-hole work

groundwater is central

Water is removed during drilling and displaced during concreting. The shaft extends substantially deeper than the visible casing.

Important: This journal separates direct visual observations from engineering interpretation. Exact shaft diameters, depths, capacities, geotechnical layers and the final cap/mat design require project drawings or geotechnical records.

Observed construction cycle

How one giant drilled shaft is made

Install steel casing

A long steel tube is advanced into the upper ground using a large casing-driving/rotary-vibratory system. This stabilizes weak or water-bearing soil.

Drill deeper

The rotary rig drills through the casing and continues well below its toe. Buckets and clean-out tools repeatedly remove soil, rock fragments and water.

Manage the wet bore

The site becomes very wet because the drilling is below groundwater. Fluid inside the bore can also help maintain stability against surrounding groundwater pressure.

Lower the rebar cage

Long cylindrical cages are lifted from horizontal, guided vertically and lowered inside the shaft. Different cage sizes reinforce the evidence for different shaft types.

Place concrete continuously

A boom pump feeds concrete down into the shaft. As concrete fills the bore from depth upward, it displaces the water/fluid already occupying the hole.

Cut back and connect

Excess casing is cut away and flattened for scrap. The completed shaft head will later be tied into the building through pile caps, a mat, grade beams or a combination still to be observed.

Why the casing can stop early

The upper soils may need steel support while deeper material is sufficiently stable under the chosen drilling method and fluid head. This saves the cost and difficulty of full-depth casing while protecting the most troublesome interval.

What remains uncertain: whether the left-in-place casing is structurally credited or simply sacrificial permanent casing.

Working interpretation

What we can infer — and what we cannot yet

Observed

The foundation system contains multiple shaft sizes.

Large central/interior casings and cages are visibly different from smaller perimeter elements.

Observed

Drilling continues far below the steel casing.

The rig works through the casing and the rebar cage later disappears well below the visible casing top.

Likely

The largest shafts serve the heaviest tower/core/column demands.

This is structurally logical and consistent with their clustering, but the precise load map awaits the cap/mat reinforcement.

Likely

Groundwater is a major construction constraint.

Repeated pumping, wet spoil, slushy working surfaces and fluid displacement during concrete placement all point to a significant wet-hole operation.

Not yet known

Individual caps, grouped pile caps or piled mat?

The next major clue will be horizontal reinforcing steel connecting the completed shaft heads.

Not yet known

Exact depths, diameters and bearing strata.

Those values cannot be measured reliably from photographs. Project structural and geotechnical documents would resolve them.
